# Market Simulator

An agent-based financial market simulator written in Python.

The project simulates a simplified electronic limit-order-book market in which
autonomous traders submit orders, orders interact through a matching engine,
trades determine market prices, and executed trades are aggregated into
candlestick data.

A live Streamlit dashboard provides a visual view of the simulated market,
including price action, bid/ask quotes, spread, order-book depth, and recent
trades.

The long-term goal is to build the simulator incrementally from first
principles and use it to study market microstructure, trader behavior,
liquidity, price formation, and eventually more sophisticated quantitative
trading and agent-based market models.

# Core Idea

The simulator does **not** generate a price series directly.

Instead, price emerges from interactions between traders and the order book.

The causal chain is:

```text
Trader Strategy
      ↓
    Order
      ↓
  Order Book
      ↓
   Matching
      ↓
    Trade
      ↓
 Market Price
      ↓
   Candles
      ↓
  Dashboard
````

The market price is therefore an output of the simulated market rather than an
input to a random price generator.

# Architecture

The project is organized into four main conceptual layers.

## 1. Agents

Agents decide whether and how to trade.

Currently implemented agents:

### Noise Traders

Noise traders participate probabilistically.

When active, they randomly determine:

* Buy or sell
* Limit price
* Order quantity

These traders provide stochastic order flow.

### Market Makers

Market makers continuously provide liquidity by maintaining both bid and ask
quotes around a reference price.

They:

1. Cancel stale quotes
2. Track executed inventory
3. Adjust their reference price based on inventory
4. Submit replacement bid and ask orders

This creates a feedback mechanism between executions and future liquidity.

---

## 2. Matching Engine

The order book maintains resting buy and sell orders.

Orders follow **price-time priority**.

For bids:

```text
Higher price = higher priority
```

For asks:

```text
Lower price = higher priority
```

Orders at the same price are processed in FIFO order.

For example:

```text
Bid #1: BUY 5 @ $100.00
Bid #2: BUY 7 @ $100.00
Bid #3: BUY 4 @ $100.00
```

If an incoming sell order sells 8 shares at $100.00:

```text
Bid #1 fills 5
Bid #2 fills 3
Bid #3 remains untouched
```

The engine also supports partial executions and resting residual quantities.

# Price Formation

Prices are represented internally as integer cents.

For example:

```text
$100.00 → 10000
$99.17  → 9917
```

This avoids unnecessary floating-point issues when representing monetary
prices.

The current market price is the price of the latest execution:

```text
last_price = latest trade price
```

A buy order can execute when its limit price is greater than or equal to the
best available ask.

A sell order can execute when its limit price is less than or equal to the best
available bid.

Crossing limit orders therefore behave like marketable limit orders.

---

# Bid-Ask Spread

The best bid is:

```text
highest resting buy price
```

The best ask is:

```text
lowest resting sell price
```

The spread is:

```text
spread = best ask - best bid
```

For example:

```text
Best Bid: $99.17
Best Ask: $99.19

Spread = $0.02
```

The spread is generated by the state of the simulated order book.

# Market-Maker Inventory

Each market maker tracks its net inventory.

Conceptually:

```text
inventory = cumulative shares bought - cumulative shares sold
```

Therefore:

```text
inventory > 0 → long
inventory < 0 → short
inventory = 0 → flat
```

Market makers modify their quotes according to inventory.

A simplified reservation-price rule is currently used:

```text
adjusted_price = market_price - inventory_skew × inventory
```

or mathematically:

```text
P* = P - kI
```

where:

* `P` = reference market price
* `I` = market-maker inventory
* `k` = inventory sensitivity
* `P*` = inventory-adjusted reference price

If a market maker becomes too long, its quotes move downward.

This makes it:

* less aggressive when buying
* more aggressive when selling

If the market maker becomes short, the opposite occurs.

This produces the feedback loop:

```text
Trades
   ↓
Inventory
   ↓
Quote Adjustment
   ↓
Order Book
   ↓
Future Trades
```

# Simulation Time

The market operates using discrete simulation ticks.

For example:

```text
Tick 0
Tick 1
Tick 2
...
Tick 1000
```

A tick currently represents a simulation step rather than a fixed amount of
real-world time.

During each step:

```text
Noise traders act
        ↓
Orders may be submitted
        ↓
Market makers process inventory
        ↓
Market makers refresh quotes
        ↓
Orders may execute
        ↓
Market-maker inventory is updated
        ↓
Market advances one tick
```

---

# Candlestick Generation

Trades are aggregated into OHLCV candles.

By default:

```text
10 simulation ticks = 1 candle
```

Each candle contains:

* Open
* High
* Low
* Close
* Volume
* Trade count
* Start tick
* End tick

For a collection of trades:

```text
Open  = first execution price
High  = highest execution price
Low   = lowest execution price
Close = final execution price
Volume = total executed quantity
```

If no trade occurs during a candle interval, the candle remains continuous
using the previous market price with zero volume.

Therefore:

```text
1000 ticks / 10 ticks per candle = 100 candles
```

even when some intervals contain no executions.

# Current Model Limitations

This project is intentionally a simplified market model.

It should **not** currently be interpreted as a calibrated model of a real
financial exchange.

Important limitations include:

* Simplified trader behavior
* Simplified market-maker strategy
* No explicit trader cash balances
* No full P&L accounting
* No margin or leverage
* No fundamental asset value
* No information or news process
* No explicit market-order type
* Simplified cancellation behavior
* No transaction fees or exchange rebates
* No latency
* No trading sessions
* No exchange-specific tick-size rules
* No realistic calibration to empirical order-flow distributions
* No volatility regime model
* No sophisticated risk management
* No realistic institutional execution algorithms

The fact that generated prices visually resemble financial charts does not
demonstrate that the simulator reproduces real financial-market dynamics.

The objective is to build those mechanisms explicitly and test their effects.

# Design Philosophy

The project is being developed from first principles.

Instead of generating realistic-looking price charts directly, the goal is to
model mechanisms that can produce market behavior:

```text
Agents
  ↓
Decisions
  ↓
Orders
  ↓
Liquidity
  ↓
Matching
  ↓
Executions
  ↓
Prices
  ↓
Market Statistics
```

Every new feature should therefore answer two questions:

1. What market mechanism does this represent?
2. What observable behavior should change because of it?

The simulator is primarily an educational and experimental system for
understanding how market structure and agent behavior can produce aggregate
financial-market dynamics.
